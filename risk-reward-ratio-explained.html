Risk Reward Ratio in Forex: How to Calculate It and Why It Matters

By Forex Profit Pathways · Updated 30 October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: The risk reward ratio compares how much you risk on a trade with how much you aim to gain. Risking 30 pips to make 60 pips is a 1:2 ratio. A better ratio means you can be profitable even when you lose more trades than you win.

Most new traders focus on how often they win. Experienced traders look at something more important: how much they win compared with how much they lose. That is what the risk reward ratio measures.

What is the risk reward ratio?

It is the distance from your entry to your take profit, compared with the distance from your entry to your stop loss.

Risk reward ratio = potential reward / potential risk

How to calculate it with an example

You buy EUR/USD at 1.0850.

  • Stop loss at 1.0820, so your risk is 30 pips.
  • Take profit at 1.0910, so your reward is 60 pips.

Reward / risk = 60 / 30 = 2. This is written as 1:2, meaning you aim to make 2 units of profit for every 1 unit you risk. Traders also call this "2R".

What win rate do you need?

This is why the ratio matters so much. The better your reward compared to your risk, the lower your win rate can be while you still break even.

Break-even win rate = 1 / (1 + reward-to-risk)

These numbers ignore spreads and commissions, which push the real break-even point a little higher.

Expectancy: the number that really matters

Expectancy tells you how much you make on average per trade, measured in R (your risk per trade).

Expectancy = (win rate × average reward in R) - (loss rate × 1)

Example: a 40% win rate with a 1:2 ratio gives (0.40 × 2) - (0.60 × 1) = 0.80 - 0.60 = +0.20R per trade. If you risk $10 per trade, that is about $2 per trade on average, before costs. Now compare a 60% win rate with a 1:0.5 ratio: (0.60 × 0.5) - (0.40 × 1) = -0.10R. This loses money even though it wins most of the time.

Is a higher ratio always better?

No. A very far take profit is hit less often. If you push the target from 1:2 to 1:5 without a reason on the chart, your win rate usually falls. The target should be placed at a realistic level, such as a previous high, a support or resistance area, not at a number you like.

How to use the ratio before every trade

  1. Find your entry and place the stop loss where your idea is proven wrong.
  2. Find a realistic target from the chart.
  3. Calculate the ratio. If it is below your minimum (many traders use 1:1.5 or 1:2), skip the trade.
  4. Calculate your position size so a stop loss costs about 1% of your account. See what lot size is in forex and use the Lot Size Calculator.

Common mistakes

  • Moving the stop loss away to keep a losing trade alive. This ruins your planned ratio.
  • Cutting winners early while letting losers run, which gives you a bad ratio in practice.
  • Ignoring the spread. On a short 10 pip stop, a 2 pip spread is a large part of your risk.
  • Judging a strategy on a few trades. Track at least 50 to 100 trades in a trade journal before you trust your numbers.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good risk reward ratio in forex?

Many traders aim for at least 1:1.5 or 1:2, but the right ratio depends on your win rate. A strategy with a high win rate can work with a lower ratio.

Can I be profitable with a 1:1 ratio?

Yes, if you win more than about 50% of your trades after costs. Because spreads and commissions exist, you need a little more than 50%.

Is 1:3 risk reward realistic?

It can be, but targets that are far away are hit less often. Use levels from the chart, not a fixed multiple.

Is risk reward the same as risk per trade?

No. Risk reward compares reward to risk on one trade. Risk per trade is the amount of your account you are willing to lose on that trade, such as 1%.
